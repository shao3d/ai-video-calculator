The difference is not the length but the details: how many characters there are, whether your product is on screen, whether the characters speak, how complex the action is. IP development — from idea to pilot

IP (intellectual property) is your own story that can be developed into a series, a film or a franchise. We go through the whole development path: idea → logline → synopsis → project bible (world, characters, arcs, season plan) → pilot script → production of the pilot episode. The pilot is the first episode that producers and platforms use to decide whether to greenlight the project. We have filled in values for a 15-minute pilot; adjust them in step 3.

A trailer is a separate, difficult task

A trailer is a preview of a film that often does not exist yet. If there is no film script, we invent the world and the story; if there is one, we write the trailer script from it. Then we develop every character, location and costume. A 2–3 minute trailer can move through 20 or more scenes, and every one has to be staged separately. What CGI is and what it has to do with AI

CGI (computer-generated imagery) is anything in the frame created by a computer rather than shot with a camera: explosions in blockbusters, dragons, cities that do not exist. It used to take 3D artists weeks.

In AI video the entire frame is computer-generated: the model draws it from scratch from our description. So the question is not "will there be CGI" but how hard it is for the model to draw it. The model is like a very talented but unpredictable camera operator: it gets simple things right in ten attempts and complex ones in forty — and then it still needs cleaning up by hand.

Simple scene · from 12 takesA landscape, a person walking down the street, a slow camera move, atmospheric shots.

Hybrid production: live action

Hybrid production is when AI generation is combined with real footage. For example, a real artist sings inside an AI world, or a real face is transferred onto a generated character. That is how you get realism that pure generation cannot deliver yet.

What facial performance capture is

Facial performance capture is filming the performer's face with tracking markers or a dedicated camera. The computer reads every movement of the lips, eyebrows and eyes and transfers it onto the character. It is the standard for realistic lip sync in music videos and emotional close-ups.

Prices for live action and facial capture are "from" figures. The total depends on the city, the studio, the crew, the equipment and the props; we calculate it after the brief.

How the price adds up

Pre-production

15–40% of the budget, more on short videos

The idea, the script, the style, the development of characters and locations. Quality is built here: without this work the model produces random images, not your film.

Generation

25–35% of the budget

The model generates video in 15-second segments. Every segment takes a minimum of 12 takes, and complex scenes take 30–40 — we pick the best of them. We pay both for credits and for the operator's hours.

Post-production

25–35% of the budget

Editing, fixing artifacts (extra fingers, "melting" faces), color grading, sound, graphics. This is what turns a set of clips into a film.

Production management

10–20% of the budget

Communication, deadlines, two rounds of revisions and a re-generation reserve — insurance against the unpredictability of AI, so the price does not grow mid-project.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it so expensive if the model makes the video?

The model is a tool, like a camera. A camera also "shoots by itself", but a crew makes the film. Generation is about a third of the budget. The rest is the script, character development, selecting from hundreds of takes, manual polishing and sound. For comparison: a minute of traditional advertising production in 2026 costs from $1,500 to $10,000 and above, and an agency project averages more than $40,000.

What is a "take" and why are there so many?

The model redraws every frame from scratch and each time slightly differently. In one take the character's face melts, in another the camera jerks, in a third everything is perfect. We generate several variants of every segment and pick the best one. The calculator allows a minimum — 12 takes per segment for any format. In real work a single segment often takes 25–40 takes, especially with a character speaking, your product or action on screen. That is why the calculator figure is a lower bound, and the realistic budget is shown next to it.

How does native 4K differ from an upscale?

With native 4K the model draws every frame in 4K from the start, so fine details (hair, fabric, text in the distance) are genuinely resolved. With an upscale we generate at 1080p and enlarge it with a dedicated program (Topaz): it reconstructs the details, but only approximately. From 1080p to 4K the upscale works well, and on a phone or laptop screen the difference is almost invisible. On TV, in a cinema and on large LED walls native 4K is noticeably cleaner. Native generation costs 2.5 times more per take, and there are at least 12 takes per segment, so an upscale is a reasonable compromise for most tasks.

What is included in a revision round?

One round is one consolidated list of comments from you: editing, color, sound, replacing individual shots. Two rounds are included. If the idea itself changes after the script is approved, or new scenes appear, that is new work and is priced separately.

Can it be cheaper?

Yes — simplify whatever affects the price the most: fewer characters, fewer complex scenes, the "Advertising" level instead of "Cinema", 1080p instead of 4K. The calculator will show the difference immediately. For large projects we start with a pilot — a short fragment so you can see the style before committing to the full budget.

Who owns the rights to the video?

After full payment, the rights are yours, within the selected platforms. For TV, outdoor advertising and theatrical release the rights are broader, which is why the price is higher.
